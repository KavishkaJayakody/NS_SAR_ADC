Comparator Sizing Analysis

1. Introduction

The comparator is the most critical decision-making component within a Successive Approximation Register (SAR) ADC core. Its primary function is to accurately evaluate the differential voltage across the capacitor array (CDAC) top plates to determine which node holds the higher potential. Crucially, this evaluation must be performed while strictly conserving the sampled charge on the top plates. Therefore, designing a highly accurate and robust comparator is essential to the overall precision and performance of the ADC.

To meet these requirements, we have opted for a multi-stage comparator architecture consisting of a preamplifier, a StrongARM latch (dynamic latch), and an SR (Set-Reset) latch. Each stage is carefully tailored to address specific design challenges, which are detailed in the subsequent sections.

2. StrongARM Latch

The core decision element is implemented using a StrongARM latch, chosen for its proven effectiveness, fast regeneration speed, and ability to provide a full rail-to-rail digital output. The topology primarily consists of two cross-coupled inverters driven by an input differential pair, along with clocked reset switches to initialize the latch before each comparison phase.

While a dynamic latch can be optimized for speed, low offset, and low power consumption, dynamic latches inherently suffer from input-referred offset, including signal-dependent dynamic offsets that can occur even in offset-optimized layouts. Sizing the input differential pair demands the utmost care, as it directly controls the critical trade-offs between speed, offset variance, and power consumption.

We are interested in the following design parameters:

2.1.1 Design Spec Definitions

  • Input offset: \( 1 \text{ LSB} = \frac{1.8}{2^8} = 7 \text{ mV} \), but we will keep the offset less than \( 7 \text{ mV} \).
  • Clock rate: 96 kHz → 100 kHz.
  • Power consumption (current): < 5 μA.

We will begin the dimensioning with near minimum. The performance of the latch must be satisfactory for all corners and worst conditions. So we will choose slow-slow (SS) corner and -25°C.

Target rise time and fall times to 10 ps. (This is stricter for our design; we can easily relax that to 10-100 ns, but an initial 10 ps target will be assumed).

2.1.4 Design & Sizing Strategy

The sizing was done identical for all the transistors in the signal paths and minimum sizes for reset transistors to minimize the effects of injected charges making node voltages rise (this is of no concern but implemented to make the nodes not pass \(VDD\)).

The simulations show that the settling time worsens with decrease in input common mode, low temperature and at FS corner for the implemented dimensions, low voltage. However, all the practically possible worst conditions showed a settling time less than half period (5 ms).

2.1.5 Power Consumption Analysis

The design shows peak dynamic current consumption of \(300\) uA. But at the extreme current making conditions the static current is less than 6mA and typical conditions static current is at pA range. No power optimizations needed unless parasites come into effect.

3. Preamplifier Stage

To mitigate the inherent offset of the StrongARM latch, a preamplifier stage is introduced to feed the latch. The preamplifier serves several vital functions:

  1. It provides raw voltage gain, significantly reducing the input-referred offset of the subsequent latch stage.
  2. It isolates the sensitive CDAC top plates from the severe kickback noise generated by the dynamic switching of the latch, preventing corruption of the sampled voltages.
  3. It provides a high-impedance input to conserve the charge on the capacitor array.

For the preamplifier, raw gain is the primary design target; precise linearity and settling time are not critical in this context. However, while the preamplifier minimizes the latch's offset, it introduces its own static offset into the system. To eliminate this, an auto-zeroing technique will be employed. Implementing auto-zeroing effectively cancels the preamplifier's offset, which in turn allows us to significantly relax the sizing constraints that would otherwise be required to minimize mismatch.

3.1 Design of the Preamplifier

The preamplifier is basically a differential amplifier with PMOS current source loads with a circuit configuration for output common mode regulation. The implemented schematic is as follows:

Description: Schematic of the preamplifier topology.

Upon initial simulation/calculations, the standard pfet_01v8 devices were not suitable for the PMOS loads since the threshold values of the devices and hence the headroom for output voltage swings were not desirable. So pfet_01v8_lvt devices were used. Further, to improve the headroom, the tail current sources were chosen to be nfet_01v8_lvt devices.

The primary design goals for the preamplifier was to implement a differential amplifier with 10–20 gain, consume less than 20 mA, and input common mode voltage at 0.9 V. Linearity and settling time were not of interest. Since the preamp will be driving another set of MOS transistors, the slew rate concern was relaxed due to low output capacitance. However, hypothetical approximations showed that the chosen tail currents can drive the capacitance with ease.

3.2 Sizing of the Differential Amplifier

We will start the sizing with an initial current distribution of 2.5 µA per signal path, which gives a tail current of 5 µA. For initial calculations, we will ignore the feedback path of output common mode. We will target for a gain of 20 (10–20).

Note: For sizing, \(g_m/I_D\) methodology is used.

The gain of the amplifier is given by:

\[ A = g_{mn} (r_{op} \parallel r_{on}) \]

In general, \(r_{op} > r_{on}\) and \(r_{op}\) is 2, 3 times of \(r_{on}\) for equal dimensioned devices. Taking this into account, we will choose initial sizing as \(W_p = 2W_n\) as a typical design choice.

It is justifiable to present the above as:

\[ (r_{op} \parallel r_{on}) \geq \frac{r_{on}}{2} \] \[ g_{mn} (r_{op} \parallel r_{on}) \geq g_{mn} \frac{r_{on}}{2} = 20 \] \[ g_{mn} r_{on} = \frac{g_{mn}}{g_{ds}} = 40 \]

We will set \(\frac{g_{mn}}{g_{ds}} > 40\) and refer the tables (\(g_m/I_D\) method). The tables suggest a device length limit for diff pair as \(L > 0.3 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\) and \(g_m/I_D\) values as \(g_m/I_D > 2.14\).

We will choose \(L\) to be \(1 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\). For low power high gain applications, choose \(g_m/I_D\) values high. By the \(g_m/I_D\) tables we can see that:

\(g_m/I_D\) \(f_t\) \(I_D/W\) \(W\) (\(\mu\)m)

According to the mismatch calculation in the latch for diff pair, \(1 \text{ }\mu\text{m} \times 1 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\) will be suitable. But keeping a margin, we will choose \(W_n = 1.5 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\).

  • Differential pair NMOS dimensions: \(L_n = 1 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\), \(W_n = 1.5 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\), \(m=1\).
  • Choose the same device parameters for PMOS load as well with multiplicity 2: \(L_p = 1 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\), \(W_p = 1.5 \text{ }\mu\text{m}\), \(m=2\).

For common mode feedback PMOS devices, same dimensions can be used while adjusting the tail current to match the output common mode voltage. Tail currents for these devices were based on simulations and design iterations. The tail currents were chosen such that the output swing is symmetrical around the possible output voltage range. The current mirror dimensions were also matched to the diff pair dimensions while the multiplicity of each output/input node was decided based on the branch current ratio.

  • \(V_{cm}\) out: 1.4 V

4. SR Latch (Output Stage)

The output of the StrongARM latch drives a subsequent SR latch. This final stage is necessary to capture the dynamic decisions from the StrongARM latch and hold them as a consistent, stable logic level for the SAR digital logic. Additionally, it buffers the StrongARM latch, shielding its sensitive internal nodes from external capacitive loading that could otherwise degrade its speed or symmetry.

Description: Schematic of the SR latch topology.

Since the SR latch is not in the critical path of the comparator decision, it can be designed with relaxed performance requirements. The primary design focus for this stage is to ensure robust logic level restoration and sufficient drive strength to interface with the SAR digital logic, while consuming minimal power. Therefore, we will use minimum sized devices for the SR latch, as the speed and offset requirements are significantly relaxed compared to the StrongARM latch.
